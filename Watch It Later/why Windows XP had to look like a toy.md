For more than a decade, this was the language of personal  computing. A world of beige boxes, gray windows, and sharp corners.  A tool for the office, not the living room. And then, 

0:09in October two thousand one, Microsoft gave us this.  A blast of color and glossy gradients. The world called it a Fisher Price design, a toy. 

0:17They thought it was a simple facelift to look prettier. But  that friendly face wasn't a sign of confidence.  It was an act of desperation. This single green button wasn't just a redesign. 

0:27It was a calculated gamble, born from a decade of technical failures,  a panicked reaction to a brilliant rival, and a desperate attempt to not 

0:34be left behind. This is the story of a design that everyone hated.  A design that was objectively brilliant, but subjectively rejected. And how a series of 

0:42catastrophic failures accidentally turned it into one of the most beloved icons in software history.  This is the real story of Windows XP. And it's a story about how you create a legend by failing. 

0:53In the late nineties, Microsoft had a problem.  They were running two separate versions of Windows.  One was stable but incompatible with most software. The other 

1:02worked with everything but crashed constantly.  Maintaining two systems that looked identical but worked differently  was expensive and confusing. So they tried to merge them. 

1:11The result was Windows ME. A disaster.  Buggy, unstable, incompatible. It became a punchline.  Meanwhile, the design hadn't changed since nineteen ninety-five. Gray, utilitarian, angular. 

1:22And by the end of the nineties,  that was becoming a problem too. The audience was growing beyond offices.  Personal computers were moving into homes. And gray corporate interfaces suddenly felt cold. 

1:33Then Apple showed the world Aqua. Glossy, colorful, friendly.  It looked like the future. Microsoft panicked.  Leaked files from that time show Microsoft tried 

3:39to copy Aqua almost pixel for pixel. They called it Candy.  But something stopped them. And honestly?  I'm glad they did. Because what they made instead was more interesting. 

3:49Two internal design systems: Watercolor and Mallard. The second name is strange, right?  It was a decoy. A fake design meant to distract competitors while they worked on the real one. 

3:59Luna. Saturated blue.  Bright green. Matte plastic textures.  Before this, color that bold was only used for highlighting  active elements. Now it was the entire interface. 

4:09It was developed by an external studio that had previously worked for NeXT,  the company Apple acquired to build macOS. So this wasn't amateur work. 

4:17This was high-level design executed by people who understood what Apple was doing. And objectively?  It was excellent. The task was to make the interface more material, more approachable. 

4:27Mission accomplished. Everything became more logical, more understandable.  Functions didn't disappear, they expanded.  The Bliss wallpaper became the most viewed photograph in human history. 

4:37The sounds were consistent and purposeful. This is objective.  But beautiful? That's subjective.  And people hated it. They called it childish.  A toy interface. Too friendly, too soft, too unserious. 

4:48There was a fascinating pattern in user research. Many people said it was perfect for their parents.  Or for their children. But not for them. 

4:55Because Luna didn't just change how Windows looked. It changed  how people felt about themselves while using it.  If you were a designer, an engineer, a professional, 

5:03this interface whispered: You need help. You need simplification.  You need to be guided. It felt condescending.  I didn't experience this. I was in middle school when XP came out. 

5:13For me, it was just normal. I spent years in this system.  Made my first website in it. Built my school's website in  two thousand three, they even wrote about us in the local newspaper. 

5:22In a small city in the early two thousands,  that wasn't common. So XP is just the baseline of my memory.  I never judged it as beautiful or ugly. It was the water I swam in. 

5:32But my dad? He immediately switched to the silver theme.  When I called him to ask why, he said: It seemed primitive. Everyone had it, so I changed it. 

5:40My uncle refused to upgrade from Windows two thousand. He said XP didn't look serious.  And both of them admitted: functionally, it was better. More stable.  Faster. But the look bothered them. 

5:50Not because it was badly designed. Because it made them feel like children.  This kind of insight, how design decisions connect to psychology, 

5:58identity, and culture, this is what I write about every week in  my newsletter. If you want more of this depth, the link is in the description. 

6:04So Microsoft gave up. In two thousand one, the same year XP launched,  they announced Windows Longhorn.  And they immediately abandoned Luna. They decided to chase Apple again. 

6:14This time: glass. But then something strange happened.  Longhorn became development hell. Delays stacked on delays.  Features were scrapped. The project restarted from scratch. 

6:23What was supposed to take two years took six and a half.  I remember joking with friends that Longhorn would never ship.  We'd download leaked builds just to see the interface. And honestly? 

6:33It looked fine. But nothing as bold as XP.  Meanwhile, Service Packs kept improving XP. Stability increased.  Speed increased. Security increased.  And something else increased too. Familiarity. 

6:43Six and a half years is long enough for an entire generation to grow up with  something. And when Vista finally launched in two thousand seven?  It was a catastrophe. Slow. 

6:53Unstable. Driver issues made it nearly unusable for many people.  Nobody cared about the design because the system barely worked. So Microsoft scrambled. 

7:01They toned down the glass. They brought back familiar elements from XP.  And that took another two years. By the time Windows seven arrived 

7:08in two thousand nine, people had been using XP for eight years on average.  For some of us? Ten years.  My entire adolescence. Middle school to the end of university. 

7:17My formative years. And I wasn't alone.  So here's the uncomfortable truth. Windows XP didn't become iconic because it was brilliant. 

7:24It became iconic because Microsoft failed to replace it. The design that everyone  rejected in two thousand one became the design everyone mourned in two thousand fourteen. 

7:33But nothing changed about the design itself. The colors didn't shift.  The buttons didn't evolve. Bliss stayed Bliss.  What changed was us. We spent ten years with it. 

7:42We grew up with it. We built careers with it.  We made things with it. And somewhere along the way, familiarity became nostalgia.  Nostalgia became affection. And affection became identity. 

7:52This is what makes design so strange. We don't judge it objectively.  We judge it through the lens of who we were when we first encountered it. The 

8:00professionals who rejected XP in two thousand one because it made them feel like children?  By two thousand nine, they were defending 

8:06it. Because now, rejecting it felt like rejecting a part of themselves.  My dad, who switched to the silver theme because  the blue felt too common? He still remembers XP fondly. 

8:16Not because the design was good. Because he was younger then.  And me? I can't separate XP from the person I was becoming.  Learning to code. Making websites. 

8:24Discovering design. That green Start button isn't just a UI element.  It's a marker of my own timeline. This is the brutal honesty of aesthetic judgment: 

8:33we think we're evaluating design, but we're really defending our memories.  We think we're judging interfaces, but we're really judging ourselves. And Microsoft? 

8:41They never figured this out. They gave up on Luna immediately.  They chased Apple into glass. Then flat design.  Then Fluent. Always reactive. 

8:48Always insecure. And here's what haunts me about this story.  Microsoft had a chance to create something legendary from day one.  The design was objectively excellent. 

8:57Functionally superior. Visually bold.  But they abandoned it the moment people said this makes me feel like a child. They had the vision.  They just didn't have the courage to stand behind 

9:06it when it mattered. And the thing is, I understand why.  Because when you create something authentic,  and the world tells you it's wrong? The easiest thing to do is believe them. 

9:14To change. To conform.  Windows XP reminds me of myself. How many times have I made decisions not  from what I actually believed, but from fear of what others would think? 

9:24How many times have I abandoned something I knew was right because the consensus  said otherwise? Microsoft chose acceptance over authenticity. 

9:31And it cost them a decade. By the time people loved XP, Microsoft had already moved on.  They were too busy copying Apple to notice they'd accidentally created an  icon. But here's the strange part. 

9:42There was one element Microsoft never compromised on. One decision  they made in nineteen ninety-five and refused to change for thirty years. 

9:50The Start button. While they copied everything else from Apple, glass,  minimalism, flat design, this one element stayed. 

9:56And Apple never touched it either. In an industry where everyone steals from everyone,  why is this the one thing they refuse to copy? 

10:03Because the answer to that question reveals something that changes everything we just  discussed. Without understanding why the Start button survived when Luna didn't,  this whole story is incomplete.
